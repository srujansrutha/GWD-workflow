Field Advisor Plan

Growers upload field photos and a short form. The system counts wheat heads, pins every photo to the field, adds weather, soil and satellite context, and a local language model (Ollama) writes the report: how the crop is doing, why, and what to do next. LangGraph runs the workflow.

What I understood

A grower or agronomist uploads one photo, or many photos that cover a whole field. They also give the location, the soil, and the crop details the system needs. The system detects the wheat heads, finds where the field is, studies everything together and writes a report with a local AI model, so farm data never leaves the machine. The report says what condition the field is in, and if it is poor, what fertilizer or other action is worth taking.

IN

Photos and a form

Single photo or a whole-field set, any image format. Location, soil, crop, sowing date, history.

DETECT + LOCATE

Heads and place

The YOLO11s counter we already built, plus GPS, field boundary and photo points on a map.

ANALYZE

Field condition

Head density, uniformity, yield range, weather stress, soil and satellite checks.

OUT

A report to act on

Verdict, reasons, actions for this season and the next, and the questions still open.

Five facts that shape the design

These come from agronomy and from what the counter can and cannot do. The rest of the plan is built around them.

A head count is not a yield

Yield also needs the ground area in the photo, kernels per head and kernel weight. The photo gives only the first term, and only if we know its scale. So every photo needs a frame size, camera height or drone resolution.

Heads appear late

They show at heading and flowering, after most nitrogen and seeding decisions are made. The report must separate this-season actions from next-season actions, and say so plainly.

Numbers come from code

Doses, thresholds and yield ranges are computed by a rules engine from vetted agronomy sources. The local model explains, ranks and asks questions. A validator rejects any figure that is not in the data.

Location is layered

Photo GPS when it exists, and a user-confirmed pin and boundary on a satellite map always. Phone GPS is typically within about 5 m in the open and drifts under canopy. Many apps strip it. The report shows how sure the location is.

The counter has known limits

On photos like our training set the typical count error is about 5%. On unfamiliar farms it is about 13% and tends to undercount by about 14%. Reports give ranges, and growers can enter hand counts to calibrate.

yield (t/ha) = heads per m² × kernels per head × thousand-kernel weight (g) ÷ 100,000 e.g. 500 × 35 × 40 ÷ 100,000 = 7.0 t/ha

Photo capture protocol (starting point)

  • Walk a W or zig-zag across the field and photograph 10 to 20 points, away from the headland.
  • Shoot straight down from a fixed height with a quadrat or marker of known size in frame.
  • Same day, similar light, no shadow of the photographer on the crop.
  • Keep location on and upload the original file. Messaging apps strip GPS.
  • For drones, upload the orthomosaic instead. It follows a separate route in the app.

The counts and spacing get tuned in the pilot, using how much the photos disagree.

How the location is resolved

  1. Read the GPS and time stored in each photo.
  2. The user confirms the field pin and draws the boundary on a satellite map.
  3. Check that each photo point falls inside the boundary and that no two photos show the same spot.
  4. Geocode the field to a region, which selects the weather source, soil layer, crop calendar and rules.
  5. Store the location with consent. Analytics only ever see a coarse position.

System architecture

How the report stays honest

This is the core of the plan. The language model never works from memory for numbers. It receives an evidence packet, and a second piece of code checks its answer before anyone sees it.

How the local model is used

  • One report call per field, with a low temperature and a context window sized to the evidence packet.
  • Built with qwen3.5:9b (6.6 GB download). With a 6144-token window it sits fully on the 8 GB GPU next to the counter. In testing, 9 of 9 reports across three field situations passed the validator on the first try, at about 10 seconds each. It was not compared head to head with other models.
  • Ollama’s structured output forces the reply into a JSON schema, so the page layout and the validator stay deterministic.
  • No per-report fee and no farm data leaves the machine. The trade-off is a smaller model, so the validator and the retry loop do more of the work.
  • If the model fails the checks twice, the report falls back to a rule-based template that uses the same evidence, so a user always gets a safe answer.
  • LangGraph keeps the run state, the retry loop and the escalation branch explicit, so each step can be tested on its own.

Photos and the language model

  • A vision-capable local model could describe visible problems such as lodging, weeds or leaf colour. It is left out of the first version to save GPU memory.
  • Any such description would be tagged “unverified observation” and would never feed a number.
  • Disease and nutrient-deficiency diagnosis from a photo is unreliable in general, so the report asks to scout the area instead of naming a cause.
  • Dedicated models for lodging, weeds and disease are a later phase, trained on our own labelled photos.

Reference bands, kernel counts and sufficiency ranges are placeholders until the agronomy team sets them per region and variety. Every recommendation carries what to do, why (evidence ids), when (the stage window), the expected effect, how sure we are, and who must confirm it.

This season (heads are visible)

  • Yield range and harvest planning.
  • Irrigation during grain fill, where water is available.
  • Disease scouting around flowering. A licensed advisor picks any product and dose.
  • Late-season feeding only where the regional rules allow it, always marked for agronomist confirmation.

Next season (what the photos teach)

  • Seed rate and sowing date for weak zones.
  • Nitrogen amount and split timing, set from a spring soil test.
  • Lime or phosphorus where the soil test is low.
  • Drainage or compaction work in the zones that stayed thin.

One page for a decision, then the detail underneath: verdict and confidence, key numbers, a map with the photo points and zones, the reasons, actions split by season, assumptions, and the questions still open. It exports as a PDF.

Decisions I need from you

Who uses it first?Farmers, agronomists or an agri company. I would start with agronomists and advisors, since they can confirm the advice.
Which countries and languages first?This decides the weather and soil sources, the rules, the fertilizer units and the report language.
Wheat only, or more crops?I would assume wheat only until the first pilot works.
How will photos be taken?Phone close-ups, handheld from above, or drone. Each one changes the scale and counting method.
Can we get real yields?Even 20 to 50 fields with harvest weights from partners would let us calibrate the forecast.
Where can farm data be stored?The local model keeps reports on the machine. Any rules that decide where the database and uploaded photos may live still apply.
Web only, or a mobile app too?Guided capture in the field helps photo quality a lot, but it adds a whole phase.
